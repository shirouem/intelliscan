Problem 1: Quadrilateral Properties

Question

If ABCD is a quadrilateral with AB+CD=AD+BC, then show that whether the quadrilateral is convex or not, there is a circle touching all its sides (produced if necessary). Also find the radius of the 'incircle' of such a quadrilateral maximum for given... (text cut off)

Solution

A quadrilateral where the sums of opposite sides are equal (AB+CD=AD+BC) satisfies Pitot's Theorem.

1. Proof of Existence of an Incircle

  • Convex Case: For a convex quadrilateral, if AB+CD=AD+BC, it is a tangential quadrilateral. This means a single circle can be constructed that is tangent to all four internal sides. The intersection of the angle bisectors of at least three angles will meet at a single point I, which serves as the incenter. The perpendicular distance from I to any side is the inradius r.

  • Non-Convex (Concave) Case: If the quadrilateral is self-intersecting or concave, the same algebraic relation holds by extending the lines of the sides (producing them). The circle becomes an "excircle" to some sides and an "incircle" to others, maintaining a central point equidistant from the lines containing all four segments.

2. Maximum Inradius r

The area K of a tangential quadrilateral can be expressed in terms of its inradius r and semiperimeter s:

K=r⋅s

Thus, the radius is given by:

r=sK​

By Bretschneider's Formula specialized for tangential quadrilaterals, the area given sides a,b,c,d is maximized when the opposite angles sum to 180∘ (i.e., the quadrilateral is also cyclic):

Kmax​=abcd​

Since s=a+c=b+d, the maximum radius for a given set of sides is:

rmax​=sabcd​​

Problem 2: Trigonometric System of Equations

Question

  1. acosα+bcosβ+ccosγ=0

  2. asinα+bsinβ+csinγ=0

We can leverage complex numbers (Euler's formula) to elegantly solve this system. Let:

x=cosα+isinα=eiα
y=cosβ+isinβ=eiβ
z=cosγ+isinγ=eiγ

Combining equations (1) and (2) as (1)+i(2) gives:

ax+by+cz=0⟹cz=−(ax+by)

Taking the complex conjugate of this identity (since ∣x∣=∣y∣=∣z∣=1, we know xˉ=x1​=cosα−isinα):

xa​+yb​+zc​=0

Equation (3) can be rewritten as:

cosαa​+cosβb​+cosγc​=0

Since cosα=2x+x1​​, substitution yields a direct dependency. Alternatively, expand the system algebraically: From xa​+yb​+zc​=0, we clear denominators:

ayz+bxz+cxy=0

Substitute z=−cax+by​:

ay(−cax+by​)+bx(−cax+by​)+cxy=0
−a2xy−aby2−abx2−b2xy+c2xy=0
ab(x2+y2)+(a2+b2−c2)xy=0

Dividing through by xy:

ab(yx​+xy​)+(a2+b2−c2)=0

Since yx​=ei(α−β), then yx​+xy​=2cos(α−β). Therefore:

Problem 3: Inequality Bounds

Question

If θ is an angle expressed in radians and 0<θ<π/2, then show that:

sinθ<θ<tanθ

Solution

Consider a unit circle (radius r=1) with an angle θ in radians at the center O. Let A be the point (1,0) on the circle.

  1. Area of △OAB: Let B be the point on the circle at angle θ. The coordinates of B are (cosθ,sinθ). The area of △OAB using the base OA=1 and height sinθ is:

    Area(△OAB)=21​⋅1⋅sinθ=21​sinθ

  2. Area of Sector OAB: The area of a circular sector with angle θ is:

    Area(Sector OAB)=21​r2θ=21​(1)2θ=21​θ

  3. Area of △OAC: Extend the radius OB to intersect the vertical tangent line passing through A(1,0) at a point C. The height AC is tanθ.

    Area(△OAC)=21​⋅1⋅tanθ=21​tanθ

From the visual geometry of the unit circle construct, it is clear that:

Area(△OAB)<Area(Sector OAB)<Area(△OAC)

Substituting our derived values:

21​sinθ<21​θ<21​tanθ

Multiplying the entire inequality chain by 2 gives the final verified result:

sinθ<θ<tanθ
